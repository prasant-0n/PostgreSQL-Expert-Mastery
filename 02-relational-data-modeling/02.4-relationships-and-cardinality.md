# 02.4 — Relationships & Cardinality

> **Phase 02 — Relational Data Modeling**  
> A production-grade guide to translating business relationships into relational structures that preserve identity, cardinality, optionality, ownership, lifecycle, and integrity.

---

## 1. Chapter Objective

A relational database does not merely store entities. It stores **facts about entities and the relationships among those entities**.

This chapter develops the ability to take statements such as:

- “A customer can have many addresses.”
- “Every invoice belongs to exactly one branch.”
- “An employee may manage many employees, but has at most one manager.”
- “A product can belong to many categories, and a category can contain many products.”
- “A booking may reserve one or more resources.”
- “A price list applies to a branch for a validity period.”

and turn them into a schema whose legal states match the business rules.

The central question is not:

> “Which table should I create?”

It is:

> **“What relationship exists, what is its grain, what cardinalities are legal, who owns it, and which database constraints can enforce those rules?”**

---

# 2. Three Levels of Definition

## 2.1 Simple definition

A relationship describes how one entity is associated with another entity.

Examples:

- Customer → Order
- Order → Order Item
- Employee → Manager
- Student ↔ Course

Cardinality describes **how many** instances on one side can relate to instances on the other side.

## 2.2 Engineering definition

A relationship is a set of valid associations between rows in two or more relations, represented through foreign keys, junction relations, composite keys, or other relational constructs.

Cardinality and optionality determine the legal multiplicity of those associations.

## 2.3 Expert definition

A relationship is a business invariant over facts whose legal state space can be expressed through keys, foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, checks, exclusion constraints, triggers where necessary, and transaction boundaries.

A useful model is:

```text
Business Rule
    ↓
Relationship Semantics
    ↓
Grain + Identity + Cardinality + Optionality
    ↓
Relational Representation
    ↓
Constraints
    ↓
Transactions / Concurrency
    ↓
Observable, Testable Behavior
```

---

# 3. First Principle: Relationships Are Facts

Consider:

```text
Customer
id | name
---|------
1  | Asha
2  | Rahul
```

and:

```text
Order
id | customer_id | total
---|-------------|------
10 | 1           | 500
11 | 1           | 900
12 | 2           | 300
```

The database is storing two different kinds of facts:

```text
Customer 1 exists.
Order 10 exists.
Order 10 belongs to Customer 1.
```

The last statement is a **relationship fact**.

The foreign key makes that fact explicit.

This is fundamentally different from storing:

```text
customer_order_ids = '{10,11}'
```

in the customer row. The latter turns a relational relationship into an opaque collection and weakens database-level integrity.

---

# 4. Relationship Vocabulary

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Entity | A class of identifiable things |
| Instance | One concrete member of an entity |
| Relationship | Association between instances |
| Cardinality | Maximum multiplicity of association |
| Optionality | Whether participation is required |
| Degree | Number of participating entity types |
| Self-relationship | Relationship where both roles use the same entity type |
| Associative entity | Entity representing a relationship with its own facts |
| Parent | Referenced side of a foreign key |
| Child | Referencing side of a foreign key |
| Ownership | Business/lifecycle responsibility for a related fact |
| Identifying relationship | Relationship where parent identity participates in child identity |

Do not confuse **cardinality** with **optionality**.

For example:

```text
Customer 1 ───────< Order
```

could mean:

```text
Customer: 0..N Orders
Order:    exactly 1 Customer
```

or:

```text
Customer: 1..N Orders
Order:    exactly 1 Customer
```

Both are one-to-many, but their participation rules differ.

---

# 5. Cardinality vs Optionality

These two questions must always be answered separately.

### Maximum cardinality

“How many related rows may exist?”

Examples:

```text
1
N
```

### Minimum cardinality / optionality

“Must a relationship exist?”

Examples:

```text
0
1
```

Combining them produces forms such as:

```text
0..1
1..1
0..N
1..N
```

This distinction is one of the most common places where beginner schemas silently lose business rules.

---

# 6. One-to-One Relationships

A one-to-one relationship means one row on side A can be associated with at most one row on side B, and vice versa.

But “one-to-one” by itself is incomplete. Ask:

1. Is B mandatory for A?
2. Is A mandatory for B?
3. Does B share A's lifecycle?
4. Should B have an independent identity?
5. Can B exist before A?
6. Is the split for security, lifecycle, scale, or optionality?

---

# 7. Optional One-to-One

Example:

```text
users
-----
id PK
email

user_profiles
-------------
user_id PK/FK -> users.id
bio
avatar_url
```

The unique primary key on `user_profiles.user_id` enforces at most one profile per user.

A profile can be optional:

```text
users: 1
profiles: 0..1
```

The relationship is enforced by:

```sql
CREATE TABLE user_profiles (
    user_id bigint PRIMARY KEY
        REFERENCES users(id),
    bio text,
    avatar_url text
);
```

### Why `UNIQUE` matters in a one-to-one FK

This is **not sufficient**:

```sql
CREATE TABLE user_profiles (
    user_id bigint REFERENCES users(id)
);
```

It allows:

```text
user_id
-------
7
7
7
```

The foreign key verifies that user 7 exists. It does **not** enforce uniqueness.

The key must encode both rules:

```text
FK      → referenced parent must exist
PK/UNIQUE → at most one child per parent
```

---

# 8. Mandatory One-to-One Is More Subtle

Suppose the business says:

> Every user must have exactly one profile.

A `user_profiles.user_id PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES users(id)` guarantees:

```text
profile → existing user
at most one profile → user
```

It does **not**, by itself, guarantee:

```text
every user has a profile
```

That is a reverse-direction existence rule.

Possible designs include:

### Strategy A — merge the tables

If profile data always exists and shares lifecycle:

```text
users
-----
id
email
bio
avatar_url
```

This is usually the simplest design.

### Strategy B — application transaction

Create both rows atomically.

### Strategy C — database trigger / deferred enforcement

Useful when the invariant truly belongs in the database and cannot be expressed naturally through standard declarative constraints.

Expert lesson:

> **A foreign key is asymmetric. It enforces the existence of the parent referenced by a child; it does not automatically enforce that every parent has a child.**

---

# 9. One-to-Many Relationships

This is the most common relational relationship.

```text
customers
---------
id PK

orders
------
id PK
customer_id FK -> customers.id
```

Represent the relationship from the many side.

```text
customers 1 ───────< orders
```

Why?

Because the child row needs to identify its parent.

An order can contain:

```text
customer_id = 42
```

A customer does not need an embedded list of order IDs.

The database can derive the reverse side with a query.

```sql
SELECT *
FROM orders
WHERE customer_id = $1;
```

---

# 10. Enforcing Required vs Optional Parent Relationships

### Required parent

```sql
customer_id bigint NOT NULL
    REFERENCES customers(id)
```

Means every order must reference a customer.

### Optional parent

```sql
customer_id bigint
    REFERENCES customers(id)
```

Means an order may have no customer reference.

Do not confuse:

```sql
FOREIGN KEY (customer_id)
```

with:

```sql
customer_id NOT NULL
```

They enforce different invariants.

---

# 11. Zero-or-Many vs One-or-Many

These are different business rules.

### Zero-or-many

```text
customer 0..N orders
```

No order is perfectly valid.

### One-or-many

```text
customer 1..N orders
```

Every customer must have at least one order.

The standard child-side FK does not enforce the minimum of one.

This is an important modeling boundary.

---

# 12. Many-to-Many Relationships

Suppose:

```text
products ↔ categories
```

A product may have many categories.

A category may contain many products.

Neither foreign key belongs directly on the other entity.

Instead create an associative relation:

```text
products
--------
id PK

categories
----------
id PK

product_categories
------------------
product_id  FK
category_id FK
PRIMARY KEY (product_id, category_id)
```

Conceptually:

```text
products 1 ─────< product_categories >───── 1 categories
```

The junction table is not a workaround. It is the relational representation of a many-to-many relationship.

---

# 13. The Junction Table Is Often a Real Entity

Initially, you may think:

```text
product_categories(product_id, category_id)
```

But suppose the business later needs:

- assigned_at
- assigned_by
- ranking
- source
- notes
- effective_from
- effective_to
- status

Then the relationship itself has facts.

Example:

```sql
CREATE TABLE product_categories (
    product_id bigint NOT NULL
        REFERENCES products(id),
    category_id bigint NOT NULL
        REFERENCES categories(id),
    assigned_at timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    assigned_by bigint REFERENCES users(id),
    sort_order integer,
    PRIMARY KEY (product_id, category_id)
);
```

This is an **associative entity**.

Expert modeling rule:

> When a relationship has attributes, it is usually no longer “just a link”; model the relationship as a first-class fact.

---

# 14. Composite Primary Key vs Surrogate Junction ID

Two common designs exist.

## Design A — Composite identity

```sql
PRIMARY KEY (product_id, category_id)
```

Advantages:

- naturally prevents duplicate relationships
- directly represents relationship identity
- no extra synthetic key
- often ideal for pure associative data

Potential downside:

- downstream foreign keys are wider
- application URLs may prefer a single identifier

## Design B — Surrogate identity

```sql
id bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
UNIQUE (product_id, category_id)
```

Advantages:

- easy single-column references
- useful when the relationship becomes independently addressable

But the `UNIQUE` constraint remains necessary.

Otherwise duplicates are still possible:

```text
(product_id, category_id)
(10, 20)
(10, 20)
```

A surrogate key does not replace the natural uniqueness rule of the relationship.

---

# 15. Many-to-Many With Relationship Grain

Consider:

```text
student
course
semester
```

A simplistic design:

```text
student_course
---------------
student_id
course_id
```

may be incorrect if the same student can take the same course multiple times across semesters.

The true relationship grain might be:

```text
(student, course, semester)
```

Then:

```sql
PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id, semester_id)
```

This is a recurring theme:

> **Cardinality cannot be modeled correctly until relationship grain is known.**

---

# 16. Relationship Degree

Most application schemas use binary relationships:

```text
A ↔ B
```

But relationships can have higher degree.

A ternary fact could be:

```text
supplier provides product to branch
```

A naive decomposition into three binary relations can lose meaning.

Suppose:

```text
supplier ↔ product
supplier ↔ branch
product ↔ branch
```

Those three pairwise statements do not necessarily preserve the original fact:

```text
supplier S supplies product P to branch B
```

If that exact combination is the business fact, model the combination explicitly:

```text
supplier_product_branch
-----------------------
supplier_id
product_id
branch_id
```

possibly with attributes such as:

```text
price
lead_time
priority
valid_from
```

Expert lesson:

> Do not decompose a higher-degree relationship merely because three binary relationships are convenient to query.

---

# 17. Self-Referencing Relationships

An entity may relate to itself.

Example:

```text
employees
---------
id
name
manager_id FK -> employees.id
```

This means:

```text
employee → manager
```

The same table participates in two semantic roles:

```text
subordinate
manager
```

The roles matter.

```sql
CREATE TABLE employees (
    id bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
    name text NOT NULL,
    manager_id bigint REFERENCES employees(id)
);
```

This permits:

```text
A -> NULL
B -> A
C -> A
D -> B
```

But the foreign key alone does not prevent cycles:

```text
A -> B
B -> C
C -> A
```

Cycle prevention requires additional logic, often recursive validation or carefully designed graph constraints at the application/database boundary.

---

# 18. Self-Relationships Have Different Semantics

Examples include:

### Organizational hierarchy

```text
employee.manager_id
```

### Parent-child categories

```text
category.parent_id
```

### Referral relationship

```text
customer.referred_by_customer_id
```

### Graph edge

```text
node_edges(source_node_id, target_node_id)
```

All are self-reference relationships, but the cardinalities and business invariants differ.

Do not copy one pattern into another without examining semantics.

---

# 19. Hierarchies: Adjacency List

The simplest tree model:

```text
categories
----------
id
parent_id
name
```

Example:

```text
Electronics
├── Phones
│   ├── Android
│   └── iPhone
└── Laptops
```

Stored as:

```text
id | parent_id | name
---|-----------|---------
1  | NULL      | Electronics
2  | 1         | Phones
3  | 1         | Laptops
4  | 2         | Android
5  | 2         | iPhone
```

Advantages:

- simple writes
- natural FK
- compact schema

Costs:

- recursive reads
- subtree queries require recursive SQL
- cycle prevention needs additional care

---

# 20. Relationship Ownership

“Parent” and “owner” are not always identical concepts.

For example:

```text
invoice
invoice_items
```

The invoice owns its line items from a business lifecycle perspective.

If an invoice is deleted, line items may be deleted too.

That differs from:

```text
employee
department
```

where deleting an employee generally should not delete the department.

Ownership affects:

- cascade rules
- deletion semantics
- authorization
- lifecycle
- auditing
- archival
- retention

Therefore ask:

> “Which object controls the lifecycle of this relationship?”

before choosing `ON DELETE CASCADE`.

---

# 21. Foreign-Key Actions

PostgreSQL supports different referential actions.

Common choices:

```sql
ON DELETE NO ACTION
ON DELETE RESTRICT
ON DELETE CASCADE
ON DELETE SET NULL
ON DELETE SET DEFAULT
```

### CASCADE

Use when the child is conceptually owned by the parent and cannot meaningfully exist independently.

Example:

```text
order → order_items
```

### SET NULL

Useful when the relationship is optional and the child remains meaningful without the parent.

Example:

```text
invoice → sales_rep
```

### RESTRICT / NO ACTION

Useful where deleting the parent should be rejected while dependent facts exist.

Do not choose based on convenience.

---

# 22. The Dangerous Cascade

A schema can accidentally create a deletion chain like:

```text
organization
   ↓
branch
   ↓
customer
   ↓
order
   ↓
order_item
   ↓
shipment
```

One delete can remove enormous amounts of data.

Before using cascade, map:

```text
delete source
    ↓
all immediate dependents
    ↓
all transitive dependents
```

Production rule:

> `ON DELETE CASCADE` should reflect domain ownership, not simply make foreign-key errors disappear.

---

# 23. Multi-Tenant Relationships

In a multi-tenant system, relationship correctness usually includes tenant identity.

Consider:

```text
branches
--------
id
organization_id

orders
------
id
branch_id
```

A branch belongs to one organization.

The deeper question is:

> Can an order accidentally reference a branch from another organization?

If tenant boundaries are only checked in application code, cross-tenant relationships can appear during bugs, manual SQL, migrations, or compromised credentials.

A stronger model can propagate tenant identity into composite relationships.

Example:

```sql
CREATE TABLE branches (
    organization_id bigint NOT NULL,
    id bigint NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (organization_id, id)
);

CREATE TABLE orders (
    organization_id bigint NOT NULL,
    id bigint NOT NULL,
    branch_id bigint NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (organization_id, id),
    FOREIGN KEY (organization_id, branch_id)
        REFERENCES branches (organization_id, id)
);
```

Now the relationship itself carries the tenant boundary.

This pattern becomes extremely important later in:

- row-level security
- authorization
- sharding
- partitioning
- cross-tenant leakage prevention

---

# 24. Cross-Tenant Failure Pattern

Bad design:

```text
branches
id | tenant_id

orders
id | branch_id
```

Application code:

```ts
WHERE branch_id = :branchId
AND tenant_id = :tenantId
```

This may be correct in ordinary paths, but the database relationship itself does not guarantee it.

A rogue insert could attempt:

```text
tenant A order → tenant B branch
```

When tenant identity is part of the key/relationship, the database can reject the mismatch directly.

---

# 25. Relationships and Alternate Keys

A foreign key does not have to reference only the primary key.

It can reference a suitable unique key.

Example:

```sql
CREATE TABLE customers (
    id bigint PRIMARY KEY,
    account_code text NOT NULL UNIQUE
);
```

Another table can reference:

```sql
account_code text
    REFERENCES customers(account_code)
```

But choose carefully.

Primary keys generally provide stable internal identity.

Natural keys often have business meaning and may change.

If a referenced business value can change, ask whether `ON UPDATE` behavior and migration semantics are acceptable.

---

# 26. Identifying Relationships

A child may depend on the parent for its identity.

Example:

```text
order
id

order_item
order_id
line_number
```

A natural identity might be:

```text
(order_id, line_number)
```

The child cannot be uniquely identified without the parent identity.

This is an identifying relationship.

Contrast with:

```text
order_item
id
order_id
```

where the child owns an independent surrogate identity.

Neither pattern is universally correct; the choice follows domain semantics and access requirements.

---

# 27. Relationship Attributes vs Entity Attributes

Suppose:

> Alice is an employee of ACME from January to June, and Bob is the approver.

Where does `approved_by` belong?

Possibilities:

```text
employee.approved_by
```

or:

```text
employment(employee_id, company_id, approved_by)
```

If approval describes the **employment relationship**, then it belongs on the relationship entity.

This is a modeling question about fact ownership and grain.

A useful test:

> “Does this attribute describe A, B, or the fact that A is related to B?”

---

# 28. Relationship Table With Temporal Validity

A many-to-many relationship can become temporal:

```text
employee_department
-------------------
employee_id
department_id
valid_from
valid_to
```

Now the relationship is not simply:

```text
employee belongs to department
```

It is:

```text
employee belonged to department during period P
```

That changes the model significantly.

The uniqueness rule might become:

```text
one active assignment at a time
```

which can require range types and exclusion constraints rather than a simple unique constraint.

This is why cardinality and temporal semantics must be considered together.

---

# 29. Relationship Cardinality Under Time

Static cardinality:

```text
Employee → at most one department
```

Temporal cardinality:

```text
Employee → at most one department at any instant
```

Those are different invariants.

A person might historically have belonged to multiple departments, while still having at most one current department.

The second rule is temporal rather than purely relational cardinality.

PostgreSQL range/exclusion modeling becomes useful here.

---

# 30. Relationship Integrity and Concurrency

A major mistake is assuming:

```text
Application checks rule
→ then INSERT
```

is sufficient.

Suppose:

> A user may have at most one active subscription.

Two requests race:

```text
T1: check active subscription → none
T2: check active subscription → none
T1: insert
T2: insert
```

The application-level check fails under concurrency.

Use a unique constraint where the invariant can be expressed declaratively.

Example pattern:

```sql
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX uq_active_subscription
ON subscriptions (user_id)
WHERE status = 'active';
```

Now the database arbitrates the race.

Expert principle:

> **A cardinality invariant that matters under concurrency belongs in a database constraint whenever PostgreSQL can express it.**

---

# 31. Cardinality Is Often a Constraint Problem

Examples:

### At most one active address

```sql
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX uq_primary_address
ON addresses (customer_id)
WHERE is_primary;
```

### At most one profile

```sql
UNIQUE(user_id)
```

### No duplicate product/category relationship

```sql
PRIMARY KEY(product_id, category_id)
```

### No overlapping room bookings

Use a range plus exclusion constraint.

### Required parent

```sql
NOT NULL + FOREIGN KEY
```

The best relational designs turn business cardinality into executable structure.

---

# 32. Cardinality and Query Semantics

Poor relationship modeling creates query ambiguity.

Suppose a developer writes:

```sql
SELECT *
FROM customers c
JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id;
```

A customer with three orders produces three rows.

The relationship has multiplied the result.

That is not a SQL bug. It is the mathematical consequence of cardinality.

Developers must reason about:

```text
1 → 1
1 → N
N → N
```

before deciding whether a join is appropriate.

This becomes especially important when combining several one-to-many relationships.

---

# 33. The Fan-Out Trap

Suppose:

```text
customer
  ├── orders (10)
  └── addresses (3)
```

A naive query:

```sql
SELECT *
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN addresses a ON a.customer_id = c.id;
```

can produce:

```text
10 × 3 = 30 rows
```

for one customer.

This is not merely a performance issue. It can produce incorrect aggregates.

For example:

```sql
SUM(o.total)
```

may count the same order repeatedly because of address fan-out.

The solution often involves:

- pre-aggregation
- `LATERAL`
- separate queries
- `EXISTS`
- carefully defined query grain

Relationship modeling and SQL correctness are therefore tightly connected.

---

# 34. Wrong Implementation: Arrays for Relationships

Bad:

```sql
CREATE TABLE customers (
    id bigint PRIMARY KEY,
    order_ids bigint[]
);
```

Problems:

- no normal FK per element
- harder referential integrity
- duplicate IDs possible
- mutation complexity
- difficult joins
- poor relationship semantics
- harder concurrency reasoning

Better:

```text
orders.customer_id → customers.id
```

Use arrays when the value is genuinely an attribute collection, not when it is secretly a relationship table.

---

# 35. Wrong Implementation: JSON Relationships

Bad:

```json
{
  "orderIds": [101, 102, 103]
}
```

This hides relationship structure inside a document.

It may look convenient in an API, but the database now has to treat relationship facts as application-managed data.

A relational database is strongest when relational facts stay relational.

JSON is appropriate when the data is truly document-shaped, not as an escape hatch from modeling relationships.

---

# 36. Wrong Implementation: Comma-Separated IDs

Never model relationships as:

```text
"12,19,31"
```

This destroys:

- type integrity
- foreign keys
- efficient relational access
- clean cardinality semantics
- safe querying

This pattern belongs to legacy cleanup work, not new relational design.

---

# 37. Wrong Implementation: FK Without Business Uniqueness

Suppose:

```sql
CREATE TABLE subscriptions (
    user_id bigint REFERENCES users(id),
    plan_id bigint REFERENCES plans(id)
);
```

If the business says:

> One user may have one active subscription per plan.

then the FK alone is insufficient.

The business identity might be:

```text
(user_id, plan_id, active_state)
```

or a partial unique index on the active subset.

Always translate business uniqueness separately from referential integrity.

---

# 38. Wrong Implementation: Surrogate ID Without Relationship Uniqueness

Bad:

```sql
id bigint PRIMARY KEY
product_id bigint
category_id bigint
```

Without:

```sql
UNIQUE(product_id, category_id)
```

the same relationship can be inserted repeatedly.

The surrogate key answers:

> “Which relationship row is this?”

The unique constraint answers:

> “Can this business relationship occur more than once?”

They are different questions.

---

# 39. Relationship Modeling Workflow

Use this process for every non-trivial relationship.

```text
1. Identify entities
2. Define row grain
3. Define the relationship fact
4. Name semantic roles
5. Determine minimum cardinality
6. Determine maximum cardinality
7. Determine lifecycle ownership
8. Determine relationship attributes
9. Select identity
10. Select FK direction
11. Add uniqueness
12. Add NOT NULL / CHECK constraints
13. Decide delete behavior
14. Consider concurrency
15. Consider tenant boundaries
16. Test invalid states
17. Measure query access patterns
```

Do not begin with ORM decorators.

Begin with the invariant.

# 40. Relationship Modeling for a Jewellery ERP

Consider a multi-branch jewellery ERP.

Potential entities:

```text
organization
branch
customer
supplier
product
invoice
invoice_item
stock_item
warehouse
employee
```

Examples of relationships:

```text
organization 1 ─────< branch
branch 1 ─────< invoice
customer 1 ─────< invoice
invoice 1 ─────< invoice_item
product 1 ─────< invoice_item
warehouse 1 ─────< stock_item
employee 1 ─────< invoice
```

But some facts become relationships with their own attributes.

For example:

```text
product ↔ branch
```

may require:

```text
branch_product
--------------
branch_id
product_id
selling_price
reorder_level
is_active
```

That is not a simple FK anymore. It is a first-class business relationship.

---

# 41. Multi-Branch Relationship Integrity

Consider:

```text
branch_id
product_id
```

Suppose a product is global, but stock is branch-specific.

Then:

```text
product
```

should not become duplicated merely because inventory belongs to branches.

Better:

```text
products
--------
id
sku

stock
-----
branch_id
product_id
quantity
```

The relationship tells us:

> Product P has quantity Q at branch B.

That fact is about the relationship between branch and product, not an intrinsic property of the product.

---

# 42. Relationship Ownership and Auditability

Suppose:

```text
customer assigned to sales employee
```

If reassignment matters historically, do not simply overwrite:

```text
customer.sales_employee_id
```

A historical relationship may require:

```text
customer_sales_assignments
--------------------------
customer_id
employee_id
valid_from
valid_to
assigned_by
reason
```

Now the relationship itself is auditable.

This is a common evolution path:

```text
simple FK
   ↓
history requirement
   ↓
relationship entity
```

Do not prematurely build temporal tables everywhere, but recognize when overwrite semantics destroy business history.

|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Customer → Order | 0 | N | Customer lifecycle? | FK on orders |
| Order → Item | 1 | N | Order | child table + CASCADE |
| User → Profile | 0 | 1 | User | PK/FK |
| Product ↔ Category | 0 | N | relationship | junction table |
| Employee → Manager | 0 | 1 | Employee | self-FK |
| Booking → Room | domain-dependent | domain-dependent | booking | FK/junction/range |

This simple table prevents enormous amounts of accidental ambiguity.

---

# 44. Database Constraints by Relationship Type

| Relationship rule | Typical PostgreSQL enforcement |
|---|---|
| Child must have parent | `NOT NULL` + FK |
| Child may have parent | nullable FK |
| At most one child | PK / UNIQUE |
| No duplicate M:N edge | composite PK / UNIQUE |
| Conditional uniqueness | partial unique index |
| Valid combination | composite FK |
| No time overlap | exclusion constraint |
| Parent deletion restriction | `RESTRICT` / `NO ACTION` |
| Dependent lifecycle | `CASCADE` |
| Optional parent after deletion | `SET NULL` |
| Cross-tenant relationship | composite tenant-aware FK |
| Complex reverse existence | transaction / trigger / redesign |

# 45. Testing Relationships

Relationship tests should include both valid and invalid states.

### Positive tests

```text
Create parent
Create valid child
Create multiple children
Create valid M:N relationship
Create optional relationship without child
```

### Negative tests

```text
Child references nonexistent parent
Duplicate one-to-one child
Duplicate M:N edge
Wrong tenant reference
Delete parent with prohibited children
Invalid relationship attribute
Concurrent duplicate relationship creation
```

### Concurrency tests

Run two transactions attempting the same relationship simultaneously.

Verify that:

```text
exactly one succeeds
or
both succeed only when business rules permit it
```

# 46. Evidence-Driven Relationship Debugging

When a production relationship bug appears, do not immediately blame the ORM.

Inspect the database.

### Step 1 — Identify actual grain

```sql
SELECT ...
```

Ask:

> What does one row represent?

### Step 2 — Inspect constraints

Use catalog queries or `\d` in `psql`.

### Step 3 — Check for duplicates

```sql
SELECT relationship_columns, COUNT(*)
FROM relationship_table
GROUP BY relationship_columns
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;
```

### Step 4 — Check orphaned legacy data

For example, compare child keys against parent existence.

### Step 5 — Reproduce under concurrency

A bug that only appears during concurrent writes requires a concurrency reproduction, not just a sequential unit test.

# 47. Performance Considerations

Relationships are frequently traversed through foreign-key columns.

Therefore indexing patterns matter.

For:

```text
orders.customer_id
```

queries such as:

```sql
SELECT *
FROM orders
WHERE customer_id = $1;
```

usually benefit from an index on the child FK column for appropriate workloads.

PostgreSQL does not automatically create an index on the referencing side merely because a foreign key exists.

A common production pattern is:

```sql
CREATE INDEX idx_orders_customer_id
ON orders(customer_id);
```

But index decisions must still be workload-driven.

---

# 48. Index the Relationship Access Paths

If a junction table is:

```text
product_categories
------------------
product_id
category_id
```

and the primary key is:

```sql
PRIMARY KEY(product_id, category_id)
```

that helps queries beginning with `product_id`.

But a reverse query:

```sql
WHERE category_id = $1
```

may benefit from:

```sql
CREATE INDEX idx_product_categories_category
ON product_categories(category_id, product_id);
```

Relationship design and access-path design are connected but not identical.

---

# 49. Foreign Keys and Delete Performance

Suppose deleting a parent requires checking many child rows.

An index on the child FK column can make those checks and related queries substantially more efficient.

For high-volume parent-child relationships, think about:

```text
parent lookup
child lookup
cascade traversal
join workload
```

A constraint can be logically correct and still operationally expensive if its access paths are neglected.

---

# 50. ORM Mapping: Do Not Let Decorators Define the Domain

In NestJS/TypeORM/Prisma-style development, developers may be tempted to start with:

```ts
@OneToMany(...)
@ManyToOne(...)
```

or equivalent ORM schema definitions.

That is backwards.

First decide:

```text
What does the relationship mean?
What is its grain?
What cardinality is legal?
What must be enforced?
```

Then map that model into the ORM.

Otherwise the ORM's default behavior may silently determine your domain model.

---

# 51. API Design and Cardinality

Suppose the API exposes:

```http
GET /customers/:id/orders
```

That endpoint implies a one-to-many relationship.

But API response shape does not define database cardinality.

A response like:

```json
{
  "orders": []
}
```

does not imply that the database must store orders as an embedded array.

Keep the layers distinct:

```text
API representation
      ↓
Application model
      ↓
Relational model
```

A relational relationship can be serialized as JSON without becoming JSON storage.

# 52. AI / Vibe-Coding Failure Modes

AI-generated schemas often create relationship bugs that compile perfectly.

## Failure 1 — Every `hasMany` becomes a child FK, but uniqueness is forgotten

```text
One-to-one
```

gets implemented as a normal FK without `UNIQUE`.

Result: duplicates.

## Failure 2 — Many-to-many becomes arrays

AI sees:

```text
roles: Role[]
```

and stores role IDs in an array.

Result: weakened relational integrity.

## Failure 3 — Cascade added everywhere

The generator adds:

```text
ON DELETE CASCADE
```

to eliminate referential errors.

Result: dangerous data loss.

## Failure 4 — Tenant ID checked only in application code

Result: cross-tenant references remain possible at the database layer.

## Failure 5 — Relationship attributes placed on the wrong entity

For example, `assigned_by` put on customer rather than on the customer-assignment relationship.

## Failure 6 — Surrogate ID hides duplicate relationships

AI creates `id`, then forgets the actual business uniqueness constraint.

## Failure 7 — Multiple one-to-many joins are aggregated directly

Result: fan-out multiplication and incorrect totals.

The corrective process is always:

```text
AI Proposal
→ Identify business invariant
→ Identify row grain
→ Identify cardinality
→ Identify legal states
→ Add constraints
→ Test invalid states
```

# 53. Expert Trade-Offs

## Foreign key vs application validation

Prefer the FK for actual referential integrity.

Application validation improves UX and error messages but should not be treated as the only integrity barrier.

## Composite PK vs surrogate relationship ID

Use composite identity when the relationship itself is identified by its participating entities.

Use a surrogate ID when the relationship becomes independently addressable or has strong operational reasons for one-column identity, while preserving the natural uniqueness rule.

## One table vs one-to-one split

Split when there is a meaningful boundary:

- optional data
- security-sensitive data
- different lifecycle
- very different access frequency
- independent growth

Do not split simply because “normalization says so.”

## Cascade vs explicit deletion

Cascade is clean when ownership is strong.

Explicit deletion is safer where accidental deletion is unacceptable or lifecycle semantics are independent.

# 59. Core Mental Models to Retain

### Mental model 1

> **Cardinality tells you how many. Optionality tells you whether participation is required.**

### Mental model 2

> **A foreign key establishes referential existence; it does not automatically establish uniqueness.**

### Mental model 3

> **A relationship with its own facts is a first-class fact.**

### Mental model 4

> **A surrogate relationship ID does not eliminate the need to enforce business uniqueness.**

### Mental model 5

> **Relationship modeling is about legal states, not ORM syntax.**

### Mental model 6

> **Concurrency turns “check then insert” into a race unless the invariant is protected by the database or an equivalent transactional mechanism.**

### Mental model 7

> **In multi-tenant systems, tenant identity is part of relationship integrity, not merely request metadata.**

### Mental model 8

> **The correct relationship cannot be chosen until the grain of the fact is known.**

# 60. Final Principle

A production relational model should make the database capable of answering:

```text
Who is related to whom?
How many relationships are legal?
Is participation required?
What uniquely identifies the relationship?
What facts belong to the relationship itself?
Who owns its lifecycle?
What happens when either side is deleted?
Can concurrency violate the rule?
Can another tenant cross the boundary?
Can the database reject illegal states automatically?
```

The ultimate goal is not to produce an elegant ER diagram.

The goal is to create a database where the schema itself makes invalid relationship states difficult or impossible to represent.

> **Model the relationship as a fact. Define its grain. Specify cardinality and optionality. Encode the invariant with constraints wherever possible. Then design transactions, indexes, APIs, and ORM mappings around that model.**
